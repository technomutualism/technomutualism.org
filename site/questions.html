Questions

Straight answers

The questions people ask most, including the sceptical ones.

What is end times fascism?

It's the name Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor give, in their 2026 book End Times Fascism, to a new alliance of tech billionaires, religious fundamentalists and ethno-nationalists. Unlike older fascism, it has no shared future to offer. It expects collapse, helps bring it on, and plans who gets to survive it: bunkers and exits for the few, surveillance and walls for everyone else. Read the book for their full argument; this site takes their warning as its starting point.

Can a community app stop drones and surveillance?

No, and we won't pretend it can. What it can do is make communities less dependent on the systems that watch and control them. When a community's money, market, messages and decisions run on tools it owns, there is less to switch off from above, less data to harvest, and more people who already know how to organise together. Fortress politics feeds on isolation and fear. Mutual aid is its opposite, and it works better with good tools.

Isn't this political?

Yes. It takes a side: with communities, against power that is concentrated and hidden, whoever holds it. It isn't a party, and it doesn't ask you to join one. People of many views can agree that the tools we all depend on shouldn't be in the hands of a few.

Is this crypto?

No. There is no coin, no token, no mining and nothing to invest in or speculate on. Mutual credit is closer to a shared tab between neighbours than to Bitcoin: credits are created when members trade with each other, and all balances add up to zero. Technomutualism borrows one good idea from the blockchain world, records that can't be quietly changed, and leaves the rest. More on blockchain.

Is it communism?

No. Nobody's home or savings are taken, the state doesn't run the economy, and markets and prices stay. It's closer to market socialism and to the co-operative tradition: people own and govern the things they use together, by choice. You join because it's useful, and you can leave whenever you like.

Is it against big tech, or against technology?

Neither. Technomutualism loves technology; the "techno" is in the name. It's against one thing: the most powerful tools ever built held by a few, and used to collect rent, to watch people and to control them. The same phones, apps and networks can be owned by the communities that use them instead.

Isn't this naive? People will always find a way to cheat.

Some will. Technology can't make people honest. What it can do is take away the easiest hiding places: secret ledgers, rules that change on a whim, money printed for friends. The rest needs open governance and people who keep watching. We're upfront about the limits. Co-operatives and credit unions have worked for well over a century with far worse tools, and mutual credit networks like WIR for nearly as long.

How is this different from a co-op?

It is a co-op idea, with better tools. Traditional co-operatives were limited by paperwork, by distance and by trusting a few officers. Software owned by the members does the bookkeeping, signed records keep it honest, and federation lets thousands of small communities trade without becoming one big organisation.

Do I need a government's permission?

No permission is needed to trade with your neighbours, though the law still applies. In many countries trades count as income for tax even when no cash changes hands, and rules about credit and payments differ from place to place. A community should check its local rules and keep honest records. The software makes that easier, not harder.

Who is behind this?

This site was started in October 2026 by the people behind The BeanPool Project, to name what they had been building. We weren't the first to use the word: a few independent thinkers got there before us, and that's how it should be. A movement belongs to whoever takes it up. Nobody owns technomutualism, and we hope plenty of people build things under it that have nothing to do with us.

Can I use the word?

Yes, freely, with no permission needed. Call your project technomutualist, write about it, teach it, argue with it. The text on this site is under a Creative Commons licence too: copy it and translate it, as long as you credit it and share alike.

What about my privacy?

Technomutualism aims for privacy for people and transparency for power. Your own trades and messages should be yours. The decisions and spending of anyone with power over shared things should be open to the members. This website itself sets no cookies, runs no tracking and loads nothing from other companies.

How do I get involved?
  • Start a community. See how to start.
  • Build. BeanPool's code is open, and so is this site's.
  • Translate. These pages would reach far more people in more languages. Translations are welcome on GitHub.
  • Talk about it. Share the idea, challenge it, improve it. Use #Technomutualism.
