A movement for the digital age

Techno­mutualism

The most powerful technology on Earth is being built by a few billionaires and the governments that buy from them: surveillance that never sleeps, artificial intelligence, weapons that pick their own targets. It's being built for a future with room for only a few. There is another way: technology owned and run by communities, for a future we share.

What we're up against

In their book End Times Fascism (2026), Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor describe a new alliance of tech billionaires, religious fundamentalists and ethno-nationalists. Unlike the fascism of the last century, it has given up on a shared future. Its leaders expect collapse, help bring it on, and plan who gets to survive it.

  • Watching everyone

    Data about where we go, who we know and what we say is gathered by private companies and fused with state power. The ACLU warns that a few firms want to become the "operating system" for police data from thousands of departments.

  • Machines that choose targets

    Autonomous drones are moving from testing into combat, and militaries are rehearsing swarms that coordinate themselves. Some of the companies that sort our feeds now build the software that sorts targets.

  • Rent on everything

    A few platforms own the markets, the clouds and the app stores, and everyone else pays to use them. The economist Yanis Varoufakis calls this technofeudalism.

  • Exits for the few

    Bunkers, private islands, fortified borders, even Mars: the people building this technology are planning their way out of the crises it deepens, not a way through them for everyone.

They are betting against the future. Technomutualism bets on it: the same technology, owned by the communities it serves, used to feed, organise and look after each other, with no single switch at the top.

technomutualism (noun)

Modern technology put to work for mutualism: reciprocity, shared ownership and fair exchange. People use it to own, run and govern together the systems they depend on: their money, their markets, their land, their data, their decisions. No landlord in the middle, and the rules written in the open, where everyone can read them and no insider can secretly change them.

A technomutualist is someone who builds or uses such systems. The word is free for anyone to use.

The idea is old. People have shared risk and pooled savings for as long as there have been neighbours: burial societies, credit unions, co-operatives, grain stores, barn raisings. That tradition is called mutualism: people helping each other as equals, not as charity and not for a boss's profit.

What's new is the techno part. For two hundred years, mutual projects ran on paper, trust and tired volunteers, and too many of them collapsed under bookkeeping, fraud or burnout. Today a phone in every pocket, open-source software and cryptography can do the bookkeeping, keep the records honest and connect communities across the world. The things that used to break mutualism can now be handled by tools that its own members own.

Technology isn't the enemy here. The same networks now used to watch, sort and target people can carry mutual aid, shared work and self-government instead. It depends on who owns them.

Two futures for the same technology

One future is a fortress: a few people behind walls, everyone else watched, scored and kept in line. The other is a commons: communities that own their tools and look after each other. Both can be built from the same phones, servers and code.

The fortress future

  • Every click, place and contact is tracked, profiled and sold, or handed over.
  • Hidden algorithms decide what you see, who sees you and whether you can keep earning.
  • Machines are taught to pick targets, and the people they watch have no say.
  • Dissent can be switched off: accounts closed, money frozen, voices buried.
  • A few platforms own the markets; everyone else rents a place in them.
  • Value flows up and out of the community.
  • The few prepare their exits: bunkers, islands, walls.
  • Power answers to billionaires, and to the governments that buy from them.

The shared future

  • Private chats are end-to-end encrypted, and your community's server is run by people you can name.
  • Rules are open code that anyone can read and check.
  • People make the decisions about people, in the open.
  • Removing someone follows published rules, not a hidden algorithm.
  • Members own the market and keep what they earn in it.
  • Value circulates and stays where it's made.
  • The plan is neighbours, not bunkers: food, energy and mutual aid close to home, for everyone.
  • Power answers to the members, and communities can copy the software and carry on.

When platform power fuses with state power, people can be profiled, silenced or cut off from their money at the flip of a switch, and the decision never sees daylight. Klein and Taylor answer the fortress with a politics rooted in life on this planet, for everyone. Technomutualism is that answer built into tools: power spread out instead of stacked up, so there is no single switch to flip.

Six principles

  • 1

    Owned by its users

    The people who use a system own it and govern it. No outside landlord takes a cut for standing in the middle.

  • 2

    Rules in the open

    The rules are published as open-source code. Anyone can read them, check them and propose changes, and every change is visible.

  • 3

    Nobody can cook the books

    Records are signed and tamper-evident, so a bribe can't quietly edit the ledger and money can't be created from nothing by insiders.

  • 4

    Value stays local

    Credit is created by members trading with each other, not borrowed from a bank, so the value of local work circulates locally.

  • 5

    Free to leave

    Anyone can copy the software, run it themselves and take their community with them. The right to leave keeps every operator honest.

  • 6

    Small and connected

    Real communities govern themselves at a human scale, then link up with others, so a global network never has a global boss.

Technomutualism is a form of market socialism: markets and prices stay, because they work well for swapping things, but ownership and credit belong to the people who do the work. Not the state, and not a billionaire.

Like a flock, not a giant bird

Picture two ways a crowd can move. One is a single giant bird: one head decides and the whole body follows. Capture the head, or strike it, and everything stops. Most of today's systems are built that way, whether the head is a company or a government.

The other is a murmuration of starlings: tens of thousands of birds turning as one, with no leader. Each bird watches only a handful of its nearest neighbours and follows a few simple rules, and the shape of the whole flock comes from those local choices. There is no head to capture and no single switch to turn it off.

  • 1

    Keep your own space

    Each bird holds its own place. Each member holds their own account, keys and tools, and nobody far away can take them.

  • 2

    Match your neighbours

    Birds match the speed of the ones beside them, so nobody drags the rest. Members trade evenly with each other, with no middleman taking a cut.

  • 3

    Stay close

    Birds steer towards their neighbours, which holds the flock together. Members back each other through mutual aid and shared funds.

Technomutualism builds tools for the flock. Each community runs its own system and links up with others, so the whole network keeps going even when one part of it stops.

What could it change?

Honestly? Maybe a lot. Much of the world's hardship comes less from a shortage of food, homes or work than from who controls access to them, and by systems where the people in charge can bend the rules without anyone seeing.

  • Neighbours can trade when money is short. Switzerland's WIR mutual credit network has done it since 1934, and gets busier in recessions, just when it's needed.
  • Food stays local. Growers can pre-sell harvests and trade with neighbours without a supermarket in between.
  • Homes stay affordable. Community land trusts hold land in common and cap resale prices, so homes aren't flipped for profit.
  • Corruption gets harder. Open rules and signed records take away the hiding places. How that works.
  • Work belongs to workers. Mondragon in Spain shows mostly worker-owned firms can employ tens of thousands.

None of this needs a revolution or a government's permission. It needs people, tools they own, and a reason to start. See where it's already happening.

Planting a seed

Technomutualism doesn't claim to have answered every question about how a society should run. It's a direction, not a finished blueprint. The aim is to plant something that works at the scale of a town, prove it, share the tools freely, and let people who are smarter, braver or better organised take it further.

Right place, right time, right people.

The first working example is The BeanPool Project, open-source software that lets any community run its own marketplace and mutual credit, on servers it controls. It's free to use, free to copy and free to improve. If someone builds something better, that's a win too.
